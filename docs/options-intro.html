Part 6 · Vanilla options

Options & Black-Scholes

The right to buy or sell something at a fixed price, with no obligation to use it. Everything else in this part of the course reuses the formula you learn here.

Level 1 The idea

Until now every product was a promise: both sides must pay whatever the market says. An option is different. One side buys a right. They use it only if it helps them.

A call is the right to buy at a fixed price, called the strike K. A put is the right to sell at the strike. The right lasts until the expiry date. The buyer pays for the right up front. That price is the premium.

A put on the KOSPI 200 index with strike 900 pays you 900 − S at expiry if the index S ends below 900, and nothing otherwise. It works like insurance against the market falling.

Car insurance is a put on your car. If the car is wrecked (its value falls), the insurer pays you the difference. If nothing happens, you lose the premium and nothing more. The insurer's job is to work out a fair premium. That is exactly our job on this page.

Two words you will hear all day:

  • Intrinsic value: what the option would pay if it expired right now. For a put: max(K − S, 0).
  • Time value: premium minus intrinsic value. It is the price of the chance that things get better before expiry. It shrinks to zero at expiry.

An option is in the money (ITM) if it has intrinsic value, at the money (ATM) if S ≈ K, and out of the money (OTM) otherwise.

Level 2 The cashflows

There are only two cashflows. You pay the premium today. At expiry you may receive the payoff. Nobody knows today if it will be zero, so we draw it as a contingent (dotted) arrow.

Payoffs at expiry Call = max(ST − K, 0)     Put = max(K − ST, 0)

Level 3 The PV formula

Step 1 · Expected payoff, then discount

As with every product: PV = (average future payoff) × (discount factor). The hard part is the average. The payoff is zero in half the scenarios, so we cannot just plug in one forecast. We need the whole range of possible prices and how likely each is.

Black, Scholes and Merton assumed that the price moves randomly. Its percentage moves follow a bell curve whose width is the volatility σ: the typical yearly move in percent. A 22% vol means a typical one-year move of about ±22%, or ±11% over 3 months (22% × √0.25).

Step 2 · The forward is the centre of the bell curve

F = S × e(r − q)T
S
spot price today
r
interest rate (continuously compounded) to expiry
q
dividend yield: dividends you would miss by holding the option instead of the shares
T
time to expiry in years

Step 3 · Two numbers from the probability table

d1 = ln(F / K) + ½σ2Tσ√T     d2 = d1 − σ√T

N(x) is the normal probability table from the toolkit. N(d2) is the probability that a call ends in the money. N(d1) is the same probability, weighted by how far in the money it ends.

Step 4 · Black-Scholes with dividends

The formula every options desk knows by heart Call = Se−qT N(d1) − Ke−rT N(d2)
Put = Ke−rT N(−d2) − Se−qT N(−d1)

Read the put formula aloud: "the strike I receive, discounted, times the chance I exercise, minus the share I hand over, discounted for dividends, weighted by the same chance". Multiply by the contract size to get money.

Step 5 · Put-call parity, your error check

Call − Put = Se−qT − Ke−rT

A long call plus a short put with the same strike is exactly a forward purchase at K. This holds for European options whatever the volatility. If your call and put don't satisfy it, you have a bug.

Only one input cannot be observed: volatility. Everyone agrees on S, K, r, q and T. So traders quote options by their implied vol, the σ that makes the formula match the market price. "KOSPI 3-month puts are 22 vol" is a price.

Level 4 The three flavours

EquityOption · OTC European index options

Put European 900 KOSPI 200 INDEX|25/Nov/26: traded directly with a bank, exercised only at expiry, cash-settled on the index level. Price it with Black-Scholes exactly as above. Use q from the index dividend forecast and r from the KRW curve. The worked example below is this product.

EquityListedOption · American options on single stocks and ETFs

Put XLV 20Nov26 187.5: a listed option on the XLV ETF. One contract covers 100 shares and settles physically (you deliver or receive the shares). These are American: you can exercise on any day before expiry.

That right makes them worth at least as much as the European version. When is early exercise worth it?

  • A deep ITM put: you would rather have K in cash now and earn interest on it than wait.
  • An ITM call just before a dividend: you exercise to own the shares in time to collect the dividend.

Black-Scholes cannot handle this. Desks use a binomial tree instead. Split time into small steps. At each step the price moves up by a factor u = eσ√Δt or down by d = 1/u. Start from the payoffs at expiry and walk backwards. At every node, the option value is the larger of "exercise now" and "discounted average of the two next nodes". The calculator below has an American switch that does this with 400 steps.

EquityIndexListedOption · listed index options, including VIX

Listed options on SPX, KOSPI 200 and similar indices are European and cash-settled, so Black-Scholes applies. VIX options are the exception that catches people out.

Put VIX Option Jan27 20 settles on the VIX level on its January 2027 expiry morning. You cannot buy spot VIX. It is a number calculated from SPX option prices, and nobody can hold it. The only thing you can hedge with is the VIX future expiring on the same day. So the right underlying is that future's price F, and you price with Black-76 (Black-Scholes written on a forward, see options on futures):

Put = e−rT [ K N(−d2) − F N(−d1) ],   d1 = ln(F/K) + ½σ2Tσ√T

Example: Jan27 VIX future 19.50, strike 20, 58 days (T = 0.1589), vol of the VIX future 85%, r = 3.6%. Then d1 = 0.0947, d2 = −0.2441, and the put is worth 2.898 VIX points, or $289.77 per contract at the $100 multiplier. Using spot VIX (often several points away from the future) would give a badly wrong price.

Level 5 Desk reality

  • The smile. If Black-Scholes were exactly right, every strike would have the same implied vol. In equities, low-strike puts trade at higher vols than ATM ("skew"), because crashes happen more often than a bell curve predicts. You must look up σ for your strike and expiry on the vol surface.
  • Discrete dividends. Single stocks pay known cash dividends on known dates. Desks subtract the PV of the dividends from S instead of using a smooth yield q.
  • Premium settlement. Listed premiums settle T+1. Then the position is marked at the exchange's settlement price each day, but you do not post variation margin as with futures.
  • Contract sizes. US stock and ETF options: 100 shares. SPX: $100 × index. KOSPI 200: KRW 250,000 × index. VIX: $100 × VIX. Always multiply.
  • Expiry and pin risk. Near expiry, gamma for ATM options explodes. Physically settled options leave you with an unexpected share position if the stock closes right at the strike.

Greeks that matter

Greeks tell you how the PV moves when one input moves. They are how a desk hedges and explains its daily P&L.

GreekWhat it measuresFor a long option
Delta ΔPV change for a 1-point move in the underlyingCall: e−qTN(d1), between 0 and 1. Put: −e−qTN(−d1), between −1 and 0. A delta of −0.55 means the put gains 0.55 points when the index falls 1 point. You hedge by buying 0.55 units of index per option.
Gamma ΓHow much delta changes for a 1-point moveAlways positive for a long option, and largest ATM near expiry. Being long gamma means your hedge needs adjusting, and those adjustments make you money when the market moves a lot.
Vega νPV change if implied vol rises by 1 point (22% → 23%)Always positive for a long option, and largest for long-dated ATM options. Your main exposure if you trade vol.
Theta ΘPV change from one day passingUsually negative. This is the rent you pay for gamma: you lose time value every day the market does not move.
Rho ρPV change if the interest rate rises by 1%Positive for calls, negative for puts. Small for short-dated equity options.

Worked example

A European put on KOSPI 200, strike 900, 3 months to expiry (T = 0.25). Illustrative market data: index S = 880, KRW rate r = 2.75%, dividend yield q = 1.50%, implied vol σ = 22%. One contract is KRW 250,000 per index point.

  1. Forward.
    F = 880 × e^((0.0275 − 0.015) × 0.25) = 882.754
  2. Volatility over the life of the option.
    σ√T = 0.22 × √0.25 = 0.11
  3. d1 and d2.
    d1 = (ln(882.754/900) + ½ × 0.22² × 0.25) / 0.11 = (−0.019348 + 0.00605) / 0.11 = −0.12089 d2 = −0.12089 − 0.11 = −0.23089
  4. Look up the probability table.
    N(−d1) = N(0.12089) = 0.54811 N(−d2) = N(0.23089) = 0.59130
  5. Discounted strike and dividend-adjusted spot.
    K e^(−rT) = 900 × e^(−0.0275×0.25) = 893.834 S e^(−qT) = 880 × e^(−0.015×0.25) = 876.706
  6. Put price in index points.
    P = 893.834 × 0.59130 − 876.706 × 0.54811 = 528.523 − 480.532 = 47.99 points
  7. Check with put-call parity. The same inputs give a call of 30.864.
    C − P = 30.864 − 47.992 = −17.128 S e^(−qT) − K e^(−rT) = 876.706 − 893.834 = −17.128 ✓
  8. Money and greeks per contract (× 250,000).
    PV = 47.99 × 250,000 = KRW 11,997,883 delta −0.546 · vega 1.736 pts per vol point (KRW 434k) · theta −0.189 pts per day (KRW −47k)

One KOSPI 200 900 put is worth 47.99 points = KRW 12.0m. Intrinsic value is 20 points (900 − 880), so time value is 27.99 points.

Remember

  • An option is a right. You pay a premium today for a payoff that may be zero.
  • Premium = intrinsic value + time value. Time value comes from volatility.
  • Black-Scholes: Put = Ke−rTN(−d2) − Se−qTN(−d1). Check it with put-call parity.
  • American options need a tree. VIX options are priced off the VIX future, never spot VIX.
  • Delta, gamma, vega, theta and rho describe the risk. Long options are long gamma and vega, and short theta.
