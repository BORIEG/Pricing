Part 2 · Futures

Futures: the basics

A promise to buy or sell something later at a price fixed today, traded on an exchange and settled up every single day. Index, commodity, FX, single-stock and dividend futures all work the same way. Only the fair-value formula changes.

Level 1 The idea

A farmer will harvest maize in March. A mill will need maize in March. Neither wants to gamble on what the price will be. So today they agree a price for March delivery. That agreement is a forward.

A future is the same agreement with three changes. It is standardised (fixed size, fixed dates). It trades on an exchange, so anyone can buy or sell it. And a clearing house stands in the middle, collecting gains and losses every day so nobody can run away owing money.

Picture a bet with a friend on a football season, where you settle up after every match instead of at the end. If your team wins on Saturday, your friend pays you on Sunday. By the end of the season nobody owes anything big, because it was paid as you went. That daily settling is called variation margin.

Because gains and losses are paid every evening, an open futures position is worth roughly zero after each day's settlement. What a trader calls the "PV" of a futures position is the profit since the trade was done, part of which has already landed in the margin account.

Level 2 The cashflows

You buy (go long) 10 Nikkei futures at 38,200. Each index point is worth ¥500 per contract, so each point is worth ¥5,000 on your position. Every evening the exchange publishes a settlement price. You receive the rise, or pay the fall, times ¥5,000.

Cash on day i (long position) VMi = (Fi − Fi−1) × M × n
Fi
exchange settlement price on day i (F0 = your trade price)
M
multiplier: money per 1.00 of price per contract (¥500 per Nikkei point here)
n
number of contracts (lots); negative if you sold

You also post initial margin, a deposit the exchange holds as a safety cushion. You get it back when you close. It is collateral and has no PV of its own.

Level 3 The PV formula

Step 1 · Value of a position you already hold

Unrealised P&L since trade (before today's margin is paid) PV = (Fnow − Ftrade) × M × n

No discount factor: the money moves tonight. This is the formula for every listed future in the course.

Step 2 · What should the futures price be? (fair value by cost of carry)

Compare two ways to own the thing at expiry T. Buy it now and hold it, or buy the future. Holding it costs interest on the money (r), may earn income (dividends, foreign interest), and may cost storage. If the future were cheaper or dearer than "spot plus carry", traders would buy one and sell the other for free money. So:

General cost-of-carry rule (continuous rates, T in years) F = S × e(r − income + storage) × T

For index, FX and single-stock futures the carry formula is a hard arbitrage: the price cannot drift far from it. For commodities you often cannot borrow or store the physical easily, so y is backed out of the market price instead. The futures curve then tells you about supply and demand.

Level 4 Desk reality

  • Contract specs are the whole game. Multiplier, tick size, expiry rule and settlement type (cash or physical) are set in the exchange contract specification. CME's yen Nikkei is ¥500 × index in 5-point ticks. JSE SAFEX white and yellow maize are 100 tonnes, quoted in ZAR per tonne. CME USD/CNH is USD 100,000 per lot, quoted CNH per USD.
  • Contango and backwardation. When later months trade above spot (storage costs dominate), the curve is in contango. When they trade below (tight supply, high convenience yield), it is in backwardation. Crude oil flips between the two.
  • Basis. Basis = F − S. It shrinks to zero at expiry (convergence). An index basis trade is long cash stocks, short futures, earning r − q.
  • Futures vs forwards. Daily margining makes a future very slightly different from a forward when rates and the underlying are correlated. That is negligible for equity and FX, but it matters for rate futures (see STIR futures).
  • Dividend futures. S&P 500 Qtr Div Dec26 pays the index dividend points accrued in that quarter. Its delta is to dividends, not to the index level.
  • Roll. Positions are moved from the front month to the next before expiry. The roll costs (or earns) the calendar spread between the two months.

Worked example

On 23 Nov 2026 you are long 10 YEN DENOM NIKKEI Dec26 bought at 38,200. The Nikkei is at 38,500. Expiry is 11 Dec 2026, 18 days away. JPY OIS is 0.75% and the index dividend yield over the period is 1.80%. Assume the future trades at its fair value.

  1. Time to expiry in years.
    T = 18 / 365 = 0.049315
  2. Fair futures price by cost of carry.
    F = 38,500 × e^((0.0075 − 0.018) × 0.049315) = 38,500 × 0.999482 = 38,480.07
  3. Basis. It is negative because dividends (1.80%) exceed the funding rate (0.75%).
    F − S = 38,480.07 − 38,500 = −19.93 points
  4. Position value.
    PV = (38,480.07 − 38,200) × ¥500 × 10 = 280.07 × 5,000 = ¥1,400,348

Unrealised P&L +¥1,400,348, almost all of it already received through daily margin. Delta is ¥5,000 per index point.

The other types, in one line each

  • USD/CNH Dec26: spot 7.1200, CNH rate 1.60%, USD rate 3.90%, 21 days. F = 7.12 × e(0.016−0.039)×21/365 = 7.11058, so forward points are −94. A long lot of USD 100,000 makes CNH 1,000 per 0.01 move.
  • YELLOW MAIZE-SAF Mar27: spot ZAR 3,950/t, r = 7.5%, storage 3%, convenience 1%, 115 days. F = 3,950 × e(0.075+0.03−0.01)×115/365 = ZAR 4,070/t (contango). One lot is 100 t, so ZAR 100 per R1 move.

Remember

  • Futures are forwards with daily cash settlement through a clearing house.
  • Position value = (current price − trade price) × multiplier × lots. No discounting.
  • Fair price = spot grown at funding cost, minus income, plus storage.
  • Risk is pure delta: multiplier × lots per point. No gamma, no vega.
