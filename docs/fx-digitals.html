Part 7 · Exotics & volatility

FX digitals, touches & dual digitals

Options that pay a fixed amount or nothing. A digital looks at the exchange rate once, at expiry. A touch watches it every moment. A dual digital needs two exchange rates to behave at the same time. All three come down to one question: what is the probability?

Level 1 The idea

A normal call option pays more the further the price ends above the strike. A digital (also called binary) option is simpler: it pays a fixed prize, say BRL 1 million, if the rate ends above the strike, and zero otherwise. One cent above the line or ten reais above, the prize is the same.

A one-touch pays the prize if the rate touches a level at any moment before expiry, even if it falls back later. A dual digital pays only if two conditions on two different currency pairs are both true at expiry.

A digital is a bet that your team is winning when the final whistle blows. A one-touch is a bet that your team leads at any point in the match. The touch is easier to win, so it costs more. A dual digital is a bet that your team wins and it rains on match day. How likely that is depends on whether the two things tend to happen together.

You need FX vanilla options (Garman-Kohlhagen) for this page. Everything here reuses its d2.

Level 2 The cashflows

The buyer pays a premium today. At expiry there is at most one payment, of a size known in advance. What is unknown is whether it happens. That makes the payout a contingent cashflow.

Level 3 The PV formula

Digital: price = discounted probability

If a game pays a prize P with probability p, its fair price is p × P, discounted to today. In Garman-Kohlhagen, the risk-neutral probability that spot ends above K is N(d2):

Cash-or-nothing digital paying P in the domestic currency Call: PV = P · Dd · N(d2)     Put: PV = P · Dd · N(−d2)
d2 = ln(S/K) + (rd − rf − ½σ²)Tσ√T
S, K
spot and strike (BRL per 1 EUR)
rd, rf
domestic (BRL) and foreign (EUR) interest rates, continuously compounded
Dd
domestic discount factor e−rdT
σ, T
volatility and years to expiry

The smile correction: a digital is a very tight call spread

Buy a call struck just below K and sell one just above. If spot ends above both, you earn the gap between them. Scale it up so the gap equals the prize and you have built a digital:

Replication, h small Digital ≈ P × Call(K − h) − Call(K + h)2h  =  P·Dd·N(d2) − P × Vegacall × (smile slope at K)

The two calls are priced with their own smile vols. When the smile rises for higher strikes (common in USD/EM pairs like EUR/BRL), the call above costs relatively more, so the digital call is cheaper than the flat-vol N(d2) formula says. Desks always price digitals off the smile this way.

One-touch: probability of ever reaching the barrier

Write b = ln(H/S) for the distance to the barrier H and ν = rd − rf − ½σ² for the drift of log-spot. A mathematical shortcut called the reflection principle counts every path that crosses the barrier and then falls back. It gives a closed form for an upper barrier, paid at expiry:

One-touch, upper barrier H > S, prize P paid at expiry (Reiner–Rubinstein) PV = P · Dd · [ N( −b + νTσ√T ) + e2νb/σ² · N( −b − νTσ√T ) ]

The first term is exactly the digital at K = H. The second counts paths that touched and came back. With no drift the two terms are equal, which is the trader's rule of thumb: a touch costs about twice the digital. A strong drift towards or away from the barrier moves this ratio. For a lower barrier, flip the signs: use N((b − νT)/σ√T) + e2νb/σ²N((b + νT)/σ√T) with b < 0.

Dual digital: a joint probability

Two pairs, A and B, with forwards FA, FB. Each condition alone has a probability from its own d2. Both together need the bivariate normal N2(x, y, ρ): the chance that two bell-curve numbers with correlation ρ are below x and y at the same time.

Dual digital paying P if both conditions hold at expiry PV = P · D · N2( sAd2,A , sBd2,B , sAsBρ )    d2 = ln(F/K) − ½σ²Tσ√T
s
+1 for a call condition (above K), −1 for a put condition (below K)
ρ
correlation between the daily moves of the two pairs

If the two conditions "fight" the correlation, the joint chance is lower than the product of the single chances, and the dual digital is cheap. That cheapness is why clients buy them.

Dual digital with a barrier: Monte Carlo

The type adds barrier monitoring to the dual digital. Depending on the term sheet, a level is watched during the life and can cancel (knock out) the payout. Once two pairs and a path-dependent condition are combined, there is no neat formula. Desks use Monte Carlo:

  1. Simulate thousands of possible daily paths for both pairs. Each day, draw two correlated random moves with the right vols and correlation.
  2. On each path, check the barrier every day and both conditions at expiry.
  3. The fraction of paths that pay is the probability. Price = P × D × that fraction.

Level 4 Desk reality

  • Reading the trade description. Touch 6.812 B1:6.812 EUR:BRL|16/Mar/27 is a touch with barrier B1 at 6.812 on EUR/BRL expiring 16 Mar 2027. DualDigital Put 7.9 EUR:CNH Call 1.72 EUR:AUD pays if EUR/CNH < 7.9 and EUR/AUD > 1.72 at expiry.
  • Smile and vanna-volga. Touches are priced with smile-aware models (local-stochastic vol, or the market shortcut "vanna-volga"). The flat-vol formula above is the starting point traders quote against.
  • Pay-at-hit or pay-at-expiry. Some touches pay as soon as the barrier is hit. They are worth slightly more when rates are positive, because the money arrives earlier.
  • Barrier monitoring. Continuous monitoring (any moment, any market) is the default in FX. Daily-fixing monitoring makes touching slightly less likely. A standard fix shifts the barrier away by 0.5826 × σ × √(1 day).
  • Quanto payout. A dual digital on EUR/CNH and EUR/AUD that pays in USD needs a small quanto adjustment to the forwards. The calculator ignores it.
  • Pin risk. Near expiry, with spot sitting at the strike, the digital's delta becomes enormous. Desks smooth this by booking the digital as a slightly wider call spread ("over-hedging").

Worked example

A · Digital and one-touch on EUR/BRL

Illustrative market on 23 Nov 2026: spot 6.30, barrier and strike 6.812, 113 days to 16 Mar 2027, BRL rate 13.5%, EUR rate 2.0%, vol 14%. The prize is BRL 1,000,000 paid at expiry.

  1. Time, drift, distance and discount factor.
    T = 113/365 = 0.30959 ν = 0.135 − 0.02 − ½(0.14)² = 0.10520 σ√T = 0.14 × √0.30959 = 0.077897 b = ln(6.812/6.30) = 0.078136 D = e^(−0.135×0.30959) = 0.959067
  2. Digital call probability and price.
    d2 = (−0.078136 + 0.10520×0.30959) / 0.077897 = −0.58497 N(d2) = 0.279284 Digital = 1,000,000 × 0.959067 × 0.279284 = BRL 267,852
  3. The touch's extra term: paths that touched and came back.
    e^(2×0.10520×0.078136/0.14²) = 2.313514 N((−0.078136 − 0.032569)/0.077897) = N(−1.42118) = 0.077634 extra = 2.313514 × 0.077634 = 0.179607
  4. One-touch price.
    Touch = 1,000,000 × 0.959067 × (0.279284 + 0.179607) = BRL 440,107

Digital BRL 267,852, one-touch BRL 440,107. The ratio is 1.64 rather than 2. The high BRL carry pushes the EUR/BRL forward towards the barrier, so ending above it is relatively likely and the "touched but fell back" bonus is smaller.

B · Dual digital on EUR/CNH and EUR/AUD

The trade pays EUR 1,000,000 on 18 Dec 2026 (25 days) if EUR/CNH < 7.90 and EUR/AUD > 1.72. Forwards are 8.00 and 1.75, vols 6% and 8%, correlation +0.50 (both pairs rise when the euro rallies), EUR rate 2%.

  1. Each d2. Here T = 25/365 = 0.068493.
    d2,A = (ln(8.00/7.90) − ½·0.06²·T) / (0.06·√T) = 0.79321 d2,B = (ln(1.75/1.72) − ½·0.08²·T) / (0.08·√T) = 0.81542
  2. Single probabilities. The put on A uses −d2,A.
    P(A < 7.90) = N(−0.79321) = 0.213829 P(B > 1.72) = N(0.81542) = 0.792583 If independent: 0.213829 × 0.792583 = 0.169477
  3. Joint probability. The condition signs are −1 and +1, so the correlation input is −0.50.
    N2(−0.79321, 0.81542, −0.50) = 0.119957
  4. Price.
    D = e^(−0.02×0.068493) = 0.998631 PV = 1,000,000 × 0.998631 × 0.119957 = EUR 119,793

The dual digital costs EUR 119,793, about 30% less than if the pairs were independent (EUR 169,245). It needs EUR to fall against CNH while rising against AUD, which goes against their positive correlation.

Remember

  • Fixed-prize options are priced as prize × discount factor × probability.
  • Digital: N(±d2). It is a tight call spread, so the smile slope matters.
  • One-touch: the digital plus a reflection term. It costs roughly twice the digital when drift is small.
  • Dual digital: the bivariate normal N2. Correlation can make it much cheaper than the product of the two probabilities.
  • Barriers on several pairs: Monte Carlo. Watch spiky delta and gamma near the strike and expiry.
