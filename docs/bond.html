Part 1 · Cash products

Bonds & bills

A bond is a loan cut into tradable pieces. Its cashflows are written in advance, so pricing it is pure discounting. The skills you learn here (yield, accrued interest, DV01, convexity) come back on almost every later page.

Level 1 The idea

A government needs to borrow $10 billion. Instead of asking one bank, it sells millions of small IOUs called bonds. Each one promises to pay a fixed interest amount, the coupon, every six months and to repay the face value (say $100) on the maturity date.

A bill is a short bond, under a year, with no coupon at all. You buy it below $100 and get $100 back. The gap is your interest.

Once issued, bonds trade every day. If interest rates in the market rise, an old bond paying a lower coupon becomes less attractive and its price falls. Bond prices and yields always move in opposite directions.

You lend a friend £100. They promise you £2.31 every six months and the £100 back in eight years. If a bank starts offering 5% elsewhere, nobody would pay you the full £100 for your friend's IOU. You would have to sell it for less.

Level 2 The cashflows

You pay the price on the settlement date. You receive every coupon left and then the face value with the last coupon. All amounts are known in advance (assuming the issuer doesn't default).

Level 3 The PV formula

Step 1 · price from a discount curve

Every cashflow is known, so the PV is the toolkit formula applied directly. This is how a pricing system values a bond against a curve.

Dirty price per 100 face, from discount factors Pdirty = nΣi=1 cf × D(ti) + 100 × D(tn)
c
annual coupon per 100 face (4.625)
f
coupons per year (2 for Treasuries and Gilts, 1 for Bunds and most EUR bonds)
D(t)
discount factor from the government curve, or from the issuer's credit curve

Step 2 · price from a yield

Traders quote bonds by a single number, the yield to maturity y. It is the one flat rate that, used to discount every cashflow, reproduces the price. The first coupon is only a fraction w of a period away.

Dirty price from yield (street convention) Pdirty = n−1Σk=0 c/f(1 + y/f)k+w + 100(1 + y/f)n−1+w     w = days to next coupondays in coupon period

Step 3 · clean price and accrued interest

Between coupons the buyer owes the seller the interest earned so far, called accrued interest. Screens show the clean price, which strips it out, so the price does not jump on coupon dates. You pay the dirty price.

Accrued interest and clean price AI = cf × days since last coupondays in coupon period     Pclean = Pdirty − AI
    PVposition = face100 × Pdirty

Step 4 · how the price reacts to yield

DV01, modified duration, convexity DV01 = P(y − 1bp) − P(y)    Dmod = DV01P × 0.0001
ΔP ≈ −Dmod × P × Δy + ½ × Conv × P × (Δy)2

Duration is the straight-line estimate. Convexity corrects for the price-yield line being curved. For a normal bond the correction is always in your favour: prices rise more than duration says when yields fall, and fall less when yields rise.

Step 5 · bills and the Z-spread

US T-bill on a discount-rate quote d, and its money-market yield P = 100 × (1 − d × days360)     yMM = ( 100P − 1 ) × 360days
Z-spread: the constant spread over the curve that matches the market price Pdirty, market = iΣ CFi × e−(z(ti) + Z) ti

The Z-spread is how credit and EM desks compare bonds from different issuers. A wider Z-spread means the market sees more default risk or less liquidity.

Level 4 Desk reality

  • Settlement. US Treasuries settle T+1, most other government and corporate bonds T+2. Accrued interest runs to the settlement date, not the trade date.
  • Day counts. Treasuries, Gilts and Bunds use ACT/ACT (Bunds pay annually). US corporates and many EM bonds use 30/360.
  • Ex-dividend. Gilts go ex-coupon seven business days before payment. A buyer in that window does not get the next coupon, so accrued interest turns negative.
  • EM sovereigns. ZAMBIN 0.5 12/31/53 REGS is a Zambian bond from the 2024 restructuring. "REGS" means it was sold under Regulation S to non-US investors. Bonds like this can step up their coupons and amortise. You price them from the cashflow schedule on the term sheet, and discount at a wide credit spread. CS01 matters more than DV01.
  • Amortising bonds and FRNs. Bonds that repay principal early carry a factor (fraction of face still outstanding): multiply PV by it. A floating-rate note pays index + spread, priced with forwards as in the FRA page.

Worked example

US Treasury 4.625% 15 Feb 2035, priced at a yield of 4.30% for settlement 24 Nov 2026. The last coupon was 15 Aug 2026 and the next is 15 Feb 2027.

  1. Coupon period 15 Aug → 15 Feb is 184 days. Days since last coupon: 101. Days to next: 83. Coupons left: 17.
    w = 83/184 = 0.45109
  2. Accrued interest.
    AI = 4.625/2 × 101/184 = 1.26936
  3. Discount each cashflow at 4.30%/2 = 2.15% per half-year. The first coupon is 0.45 periods away and the last 16.45.
    first: 2.3125 / 1.0215^0.45109 = 2.3125 × 0.990450 = 2.29042 last: 102.3125 / 1.0215^16.45109 = 102.3125 × 0.704724 = 72.1020
  4. Add all 17 discounted cashflows (see the table in the calculator).
    P_dirty = 103.49501 P_clean = 103.49501 − 1.26936 = 102.22565
  5. DV01 by repricing at 4.29% and 4.31%.
    DV01 = 0.069584 per 100 → $6,958 per $10m face D_mod = 6.7234
  6. Check convexity: yields jump 100bp to 5.30%.
    duration only: 96.537 duration + convexity: 96.816 exact: 96.809
  7. A 91-day T-bill quoted at a 4.20% discount rate.
    P = 100 × (1 − 0.042 × 91/360) = 98.9383 money-market yield 4.2451%

Clean price 102.2256, dirty 103.4950. A $10m face position is worth $10,349,501 and has a DV01 of $6,958.

Remember

  • Bond PV = coupons and face, each × discount factor. You pay dirty = clean + accrued.
  • Yield is the single rate that reproduces the price. Price and yield move in opposite directions.
  • DV01 = price change per 1bp; duration = DV01 as a % of price; convexity is the curve's bend.
  • For credit and EM bonds, discount at the curve plus a Z-spread and watch CS01.
