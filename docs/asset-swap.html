Part 3 · Swaps

Asset swaps

Buy a fixed-coupon bond and, in the same breath, swap its fixed coupons for a floating rate plus a spread. The spread you lock in, the asset swap spread, is how the market measures whether a bond is cheap or rich against swaps.

Level 1 The idea

A bond pays fixed coupons. If rates rise, its price falls. Some investors, such as banks funding themselves at floating rates, like the bond's credit or its cheapness but don't want that interest rate risk.

An asset swap removes it. You buy the bond and enter a swap with a dealer. You pass every fixed coupon of the bond to the dealer, and the dealer pays you a floating rate (SOFR) plus a fixed extra, the asset swap spread. You now own something that behaves like a floating-rate note. Your return is SOFR plus the spread, as long as the bond doesn't default.

You buy a flat that comes with a tenant on a fixed 5-year lease. You then agree with a bank to hand over the fixed rent each month, and the bank pays you a rent that follows the market, plus a bonus. That bonus tells you how good a deal the flat was. If the tenant stops paying, you still owe the bank.

Level 2 The cashflows

In the standard par-par asset swap you pay exactly 100 per 100 face for the whole package, whatever the bond's market price. The bond costs its dirty price P (say 99.10). The leftover 100 − P = 0.90 goes to the dealer as an upfront payment. If the bond trades above par, the dealer pays you the difference instead.

After that, the bond's coupons come in and go straight out to the dealer, and SOFR + spread comes back on the full 100 notional. At maturity the bond repays 100.

Level 3 The PV formula

Look at the deal from the dealer's side. The dealer receives the upfront 100 − P and the bond's coupons, and pays SOFR + s on 100. For the trade to be fair, these must have equal value on the swap (SOFR OIS) curve D(t).

Step 1 · value of each piece, per 100 face upfront = 100 − P   ·   coupons = Σj c τj D(tj)   ·   SOFR leg = 100 (1 − D(T))   ·   spread leg = 100 s A

The SOFR leg without a notional is worth 100 (1 − D(T)). This is the par-floater fact from the cross-currency page. A = ΣτiD(ti) is the annuity of the floating schedule. Setting the dealer's PV to zero:

Step 2 · solve for the spread 100 − P + Σ c τj Dj − 100(1 − D(T)) − 100 s A = 0
Step 3 · the asset swap spread sASW = Pswap − P100 × A    where   Pswap = Σ c τj D(tj) + 100 D(T)
P
the bond's market dirty price (clean + accrued)
Pswap
what the bond would be worth if it were discounted on the swap curve, as if it were riskless
A
annuity of the floating leg: Σ accrual × discount factor (quarterly for SOFR)

The ASW spread is the gap between the swap-curve price and the market price, spread out over the life of the trade. A cheap bond (low P) has a high ASW spread.

Step 4 · PV of an existing asset swap position traded at spread s PV = N × (s − sASW,today) × A

ASW spread vs Z-spread

The Z-spread is the single number Z you add to every swap zero rate so that the bond's discounted flows equal its price:

P = Σj CFj × e−(z(tj) + Z) tj

Both measure cheapness. For a bond priced near par they are almost equal. For a bond far from par they drift apart, because par-par ASW puts the price difference into an upfront amount spread over 100 notional, while the Z-spread spreads it over the bond's own flows. Level 4 Desk reality

  • Reading the trade description. AssetSwap P TII 1.875 07/15/35 R SOFRCMP-1D+0.529 % means you Pay the cashflows of the TIPS 1.875% July 2035 and Receive compounded SOFR + 52.9bp. TII is the US inflation-linked Treasury (TIPS).
  • Inflation-linked bonds. A TIPS coupon is 1.875% × index ratio, and its principal also grows with CPI. The "bond coupons" leg is therefore an inflation leg, projected with the CPI forward curve from the inflation swaps page. The formula is unchanged: ASW = (PV of projected bond flows on the swap curve − dirty price) ÷ (100 × annuity).
  • Par-par vs proceeds. In a proceeds (market-value) asset swap the notional is the dirty price P instead of 100, so there is no upfront. The spread becomes (Pswap − P) ÷ (P × A).
  • Conventions. Treasury coupons are semi-annual ACT/ACT. The SOFR leg is ACT/360, compounded in arrears, usually annual or quarterly. The bond settles T+1.
  • Default. If the bond defaults, you keep owing its coupons on the swap. You lose 100 − recovery on the bond, plus or minus the swap's value. Asset swap spreads are therefore a credit measure.
  • Treasury asset swaps. For Treasuries the spread is often negative at long maturities, a reflection of balance-sheet and funding costs. Traders call the opposite trade a "swap spread" trade.

Worked example

A 5-year bond pays 4.25% semi-annually and has just paid a coupon, so dirty = clean = 99.10. The SOFR curve is flat at 3.70% continuously compounded, with quarterly floating periods of ¼ year. Notional USD 10m.

  1. Price the bond on the swap curve: 10 coupons of 2.125 and 100 at year 5.
    P_swap = Σ 2.125 × e^(−0.037 × j/2) + 100 × e^(−0.037×5) = 102.3317
  2. Annuity of the quarterly floating leg (20 periods).
    A = Σ 0.25 × e^(−0.037 × i/4) = 4.54367
  3. Asset swap spread.
    s_ASW = (102.3317 − 99.10) / (100 × 4.54367) = 0.7113% = 71.1bp
  4. Compare with the Z-spread, solved by trial and error.
    Z such that Σ CF × e^(−(0.037+Z)t) = 99.10 → Z = 70.4bp
  5. Risk of the package.
    Spread01 = 10,000,000 × 4.54367 × 0.0001 = USD 4,544 per bp

You can swap this bond into SOFR + 71.1bp. The bond is 3.23 points cheap to the swap curve. A package traded earlier at SOFR + 52.9bp would now be worth (52.9 − 71.13)bp × 4,544 ≈ −USD 82,800 to its buyer.

Remember

  • Asset swap = buy the bond + pay its fixed coupons in a swap + receive SOFR + spread.
  • sASW = (bond price on the swap curve − market dirty price) ÷ (100 × floating annuity).
  • The package has little rate risk. It keeps the bond's spread and default risk.
  • For TIPS, the bond leg is an inflation leg. The method is the same.
