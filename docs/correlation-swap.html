Part 7 · Exotics & volatility

Correlation swaps

A bet on how closely two markets move together. You agree a correlation today. At maturity you measure the real correlation of their daily moves and settle the difference. It is the cousin of the variance swap, one level more abstract.

Level 1 The idea

When the yen weakens, Japanese exporters earn more in yen, so the Nikkei 225 often rises at the same time as USD/JPY. The two markets are correlated. Correlation is a number between −1 and +1. +1 means they always move together, −1 means always opposite, 0 means no link.

A correlation swap turns that number into a payment. The example trade agrees 50% today on the Nikkei and USD/JPY for 3 years. If, over the 3 years, their daily moves turn out 60% correlated, the buyer receives 10 correlation points times the notional. If they turn out 40% correlated, the buyer pays 10 points.

Think of two friends walking a dog each in the same park. Correlation measures how often they turn left or right at the same moment. A correlation swap is a bet, made at the park gate, on how in-step they will be by the end of the walk.

Why would anyone want that? Banks that sell quanto products, such as a Nikkei investment paid in dollars, carry hidden exposure to this correlation. A correlation swap hedges it directly.

Level 2 The cashflows

Nothing is paid at the start. Every trading day both closing prices are recorded. At maturity one net amount is paid:

Payment at maturity, to the buyer N × ( ρreal − K )    with ρ and K in correlation points (50% = 50)
N
money per correlation point, e.g. USD 100,000
K
the strike correlation agreed today (50)

Level 3 The PV formula

Step 1 · Measuring realised correlation

Take the daily log-returns xi = ln(Nikkeii/Nikkeii−1) and yi = ln(USDJPYi/USDJPYi−1), and their averages x̄, ȳ. Realised correlation is the usual statistics formula:

Realised correlation over n days ρreal = Σ (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ)√( Σ(xi − x̄)² × Σ(yi − ȳ)² )

The top measures how often the two move the same way at the same time. The bottom scales by how much each moves on its own, so the result always lands between −1 and +1.

Step 2 · Mid-life: realised part plus expected part

Part of the period has already happened. As a good approximation, the final correlation is the day-weighted average of the correlation realised so far and the correlation the market expects for the rest:

Expected final correlation, w = elapsed fraction of the period E[ρreal] ≈ w × ρe + (1 − w) × ρimp
PV for the buyer PV = D(T) × N × ( E[ρreal] − K )

The day-weighted average is exact only if both markets were equally volatile in the two sub-periods. Correlation over a full sample is a ratio, so a very volatile sub-period carries more weight than its share of days. Production systems keep the running sums Σxy, Σx², Σy² and project each one forward separately.

Step 3 · Where implied correlation comes from

Correlation is rarely quoted directly. It is backed out of three volatilities. The Nikkei measured in dollars has log-return x − y (Nikkei in yen, divided by yen per dollar). Using the variance-of-a-difference rule:

Variance of the Nikkei in USD, then solve for ρ σNKY$² = σN² + σFX² − 2ρσNσFX   ⇒   ρimp = σN² + σFX² − σNKY$²2 σN σFX

With a Nikkei vol of 22%, a USD/JPY vol of 10% and a vol of 21% for Nikkei-in-dollars (from "composite" options), ρimp = (0.0484 + 0.0100 − 0.0441) ÷ (2 × 0.22 × 0.10) = 0.325. The same idea gives implied correlation for an index from the vols of its members.

Level 4 Desk reality

  • Reading the trade description. CorrelationSwap NIKKEI 225 FX:USD:JPY 3Y USD 50 % 31/Dec/27 is a 3-year swap on the correlation between the Nikkei 225 and USD/JPY, settled in USD, strike 50%, maturing 31 Dec 2027.
  • Why the desk holds it. Quanto Nikkei products (for example Nikkei futures paid in dollars) have a forward price that depends on this correlation. Their seller is naturally short or long correlation and uses swaps like this to offset it.
  • Thin market. Implied correlation is rarely quoted. Desks back it out from composite or quanto prices, and the bid-offer is wide (several points).
  • Conventions. Term sheets say whether returns are demeaned (as in Step 1) or zero-mean, and how missing days on either market are handled. Japanese holidays and US holidays differ, so the two calendars must be aligned.
  • Regime shifts. The Nikkei/yen link changes with Bank of Japan policy. Over 2013–2015 it was strongly positive. In some risk-off episodes both the yen and safe assets rally while equities fall, which also pushes it positive. History is a weak guide.

Worked example

On 23 Nov 2026 you hold a long correlation swap on USD 100,000 per point, strike 50. It started 31 Dec 2024 and ends 31 Dec 2027 (1,095 calendar days). 692 days have passed with realised correlation 58%. The market implies 45% for the remaining period. USD rate 3.75%.

  1. Elapsed weight.
    w = 692 / 1,095 = 0.631963
  2. Expected final correlation.
    E[ρ] = 0.631963 × 58 + 0.368037 × 45 = 36.654 + 16.562 = 53.216 points
  3. Discount factor to maturity (403 days).
    D = e^(−0.0375 × 403/365) = 0.959441
  4. PV.
    PV = 0.959441 × 100,000 × (53.216 − 50) = USD 308,511
  5. Correlation sensitivity.
    Corr01 = 0.959441 × 100,000 × 0.368037 = USD 35,311 per point of implied correlation

The swap is worth +USD 308,511 to the buyer. The high correlation realised so far (58%) already outweighs a lower implied 45% for the remaining 37% of the period.

Remember

  • Payout = N × (realised correlation − strike), in correlation points.
  • Realised correlation is the standard statistics formula on daily log-returns.
  • Mid-life: E[ρ] ≈ w × realised + (1 − w) × implied, then discount.
  • Implied correlation comes from three vols: ρ = (σ₁² + σ₂² − σ₁₂²) ÷ (2σ₁σ₂).
  • Main risk is corr01. Vega and delta are close to zero.
