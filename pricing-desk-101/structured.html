Structured products & other records

A structured product is a bundle of pieces you already know: a zero-coupon bond plus some options, wrapped into one note. The skill is taking it apart. This page also covers the booking records that are cash, fees or bookkeeping rather than market products, and how to value them.

Level 1 The idea

An investor says: "I want to profit if the stock market rises over 5 years, but I must get my 10 million back even if it crashes." A bank builds a capital-protected note for them. They pay 10 million today. In 5 years they get at least 10 million back, plus a share of any rise in the index.

How can the bank promise that? It splits the 10 million in two. Most of it buys a 5-year zero-coupon bond that grows back to exactly 10 million. The small remainder buys call options on the index. If the index rises, the calls pay out. If it falls, the calls expire worthless but the bond still returns the 10 million.

It is like a meal deal. The menu shows one price, but the restaurant has costed a sandwich, a drink and a snack separately. To know whether the deal is fair, price each item on its own and add them up.

Desks book many of these as StructuredProduct, each with its own term sheet. There is no single formula. There is a single method: decompose, price each piece, add.

Level 2 The cashflows

For the capital-protected note: the investor pays the issue price today. At maturity they receive the protected amount, which is certain (apart from the issuer defaulting), plus a participation p in the index's rise, which is contingent.

Level 3 The PV formula

Step 1 · Write the payoff as known pieces

Maturity payoff of the note N × [ π + p × max( ST/S0 − 1 , 0 ) ]  =  Nπ (zero-coupon bond) + NpS0 × Call(K = S0)
π
protection level (100% here; 90% notes exist and buy more options)
p
participation: the share of the index rise paid out

Step 2 · Price each piece

PV of the note PV = N π D(T) + N pS0 × BScall(S0, K = S0, T, r, q, σ)

The bond uses the issuer's funding curve. The call uses Black-Scholes with dividend yield q.

Step 3 · Solve for the fair participation

The bank sets p so the pieces cost what the client pays, minus the bank's margin m:

Fair participation p = N(1 − m) − NπD(T)N × BScall / S0

Higher rates make the bond cheaper, which leaves more money for options and a higher participation. That is why capital-protected notes were popular in high-rate years and almost disappeared when rates were near zero.

Level 4 Desk reality

  • Issuer credit. "Protected" means protected by the issuer. If the issuer defaults, the investor is an unsecured creditor. Desks discount the bond piece at the issuer's funding spread, not at the risk-free rate.
  • Autocallables. The most common equity note today. On each observation date (say yearly), if the index is at or above its starting level, the note repays early with a coupon. If it never calls and the index ends below a barrier (say 60%), the investor loses like the index. In pieces: a bond, a strip of digital coupons that may stop early, and a down-and-in put sold by the investor. Path dependence means Monte Carlo pricing, with local or stochastic vol for the smile.
  • Reading the trade description. StructuredProduct trades carry a generic description. The payoff lives in the term sheet and in the pricing model attached to the trade, so always read the term sheet before quoting a price.
  • Hedging. The issuer hedges each piece: rates risk on the bond with swaps, delta and vega on the options with listed options or futures.

Worked example

A · Fair participation of a capital-protected note

USD 10m, 5 years, 100% protection. The issuer's rate is 4.00% (continuous), index dividend yield 1.50%, index vol 18%. The bank keeps a 1% margin.

  1. Zero-coupon bond.
    D(5) = e^(−0.04 × 5) = 0.818731 Bond = 10,000,000 × 0.818731 = 8,187,308
  2. Option budget.
    Budget = 10,000,000 × (1 − 0.01) − 8,187,308 = 1,712,692
  3. Price of a 5-year at-the-money call, per 100 of index (Black-Scholes, r = 4%, q = 1.5%, σ = 18%).
    Call = 20.0345 per 100 → on USD 10m: 10,000,000 × 0.200345 = 2,003,453
  4. Fair participation.
    p = 1,712,692 / 2,003,453 = 85.49%

The bank can offer 85.5% participation. The note is worth USD 9.90m to the investor on day one, and the 1% difference is the bank's margin.

B · A SimpleFlow

The JPY 23,000,000,000 payment on 25 Nov 2026, valued on 23 Nov 2026 (2 days) at a JPY rate of 0.75%.

  1. Discount factor and PV. We pay, so the sign is negative.
    D = e^(−0.0075 × 2/365) = 0.99995890 PV = −23,000,000,000 × 0.99995890 = JPY −22,999,054,814

PV JPY −22.999bn. Discounting a 2-day payment changes it by under JPY 1m, which is why desks treat these almost as cash.

Try it

Lower the protection to 90% and watch the fair participation jump. Raise rates and the bond gets cheaper, leaving more for options.

Remember

  • Structured product = known pieces. Decompose, price each piece, add.
  • Capital-protected note = zero-coupon bond + participation × at-the-money calls.
  • Fair participation = (price after margin − bond) ÷ call cost. It is higher when rates and vol are favourable.
  • Autocalls add early redemption and a sold down-and-in put, so they need Monte Carlo.
  • Cash, fee and bookkeeping records: PV = ± amount × discount factor, with FX if needed.
