The pricing toolkit

Every product in this course is priced with the same few tools: discount factors, forward rates, a little probability and two option formulas. Learn these once and every later page is just a new arrangement of them.

1 · Money has a time stamp

£100 today is worth more than £100 in two years, because today's £100 can be put in the bank and earn interest. Pricing is mostly about moving money through time.

If the bank pays 4% a year, £100 grows to 100 × 1.04 = £104 after one year and 100 × 1.042 = £108.16 after two. Run it backwards: to have £100 in two years you only need 100 ÷ 1.042 = £92.46 today. That £92.46 is the present value (PV) of £100 paid in two years.

A discount factor is an exchange rate between dates. Just as 1 euro buys 0.86 pounds, £1 delivered in two years "buys" £0.9246 today.

Discount factor and present value D(T) = 1(1 + r)T     PV = Σi CFi × D(ti)
D(T)
discount factor: today's value of 1 unit paid at time T (in years). Always between 0 and 1 when rates are positive.
CFi
the i-th cashflow, positive if you receive it and negative if you pay it
Σ
"add them all up"

The PV of any product is the sum of its cashflows, each multiplied by its discount factor. The hard part is only ever working out what the cashflows will be.

2 · Compounding: the same rate, written four ways

"5%" means nothing until you say how often interest is added. The discount factor is what matters. A rate is just a way of quoting it.

Example: 5% annual compounding is the same as 4.879% continuous (ln 1.05 = 0.04879) and 4.939% semi-annual. All three give the same discount factor.

3 · Day counts: how long is "half a year"?

Interest is rate × time, so we need a rule that turns two dates into a year fraction τ. Each market has its own rule, called a day count. Getting it wrong is the most common reason a price is off by a few hundred dollars.

Day countRule15 Jan → 15 Jul 2027Used for

Using ACT/365 where the market uses ACT/360 makes interest 1.4% too small. On a $100m one-year deposit at 4% that is about $56,000.

4 · Zero curves and forward rates

The market does not pay the same rate for every horizon. A zero curve lists, for each maturity T, the rate z(T) for money locked up from today until T with nothing paid in between. From it we get a discount factor for every date: P(T) = e−z(T)T.

A forward rate is the rate the curve implies for a period that starts in the future. Investing to T2 directly must give the same as investing to T1 and then rolling at the forward rate. If it did not, you could make free money.

Forward rate between T1 and T2 (simple, accrual τ) 1P(T2) = 1P(T1) × (1 + Fτ)   ⇒   F = 1τ ( P(T1)P(T2) − 1 )

Example: 1-year zero 3.0% and 2-year zero 3.5% (continuous). P(1) = e−0.03 = 0.970446, P(2) = e−0.07 = 0.932394, so the 1y-into-1y forward is 0.970446 ÷ 0.932394 − 1 = 4.081% simple. In continuous terms it is (3.5 × 2 − 3.0 × 1) ÷ 1 = 4.0%. When the curve slopes up, forwards sit above the zero rates.

5 · Two curves: discounting vs projection

Since 2008 desks use separate curves for two separate jobs.

  • Discounting curve (OIS). Most trades are collateralised: whoever is losing posts cash, and that cash earns the overnight rate (SOFR, €STR, SONIA, SARON, TONA). So the cost of carrying a trade is the overnight rate, and we discount with the OIS curve built from it.
  • Projection curves. A floating leg paying 3M EURIBOR or 3M STIBOR pays more than overnight, because a 3-month unsecured loan carries bank credit risk. Each index gets its own curve, used only to compute its forwards.

For a currency without collateral in that currency (for example a USD-collateralised ZAR swap), the discount curve is the ZAR curve adjusted by the cross-currency basis. The cross-currency page covers this.

In pricing systems you will see a "rates provider" or "curve set": one discount curve per currency and collateral type, plus one projection curve per index and tenor (SOFR, EURIBOR-3M, EURIBOR-6M, TIIE-28D and so on).

6 · Basis points, DV01 and bucketed risk

Rates move in basis points: 1bp = 0.01% = 0.0001. The first question a trader asks about any rates position is "what is my DV01?". That is how much the PV changes if rates rise by 1bp. It is also called PV01.

DV01 by bump-and-reprice DV01 = PV(curve + 1bp) − PV(curve)

Example: receiving $100m in 5 years, zero rate 4% continuous. PV = 100m × e−0.20 = $81,873,075. Bumping to 4.01% gives a PV $40,926 lower, so DV01 = −$40,926. A quick rule: DV01 ≈ PV × T × 0.0001 = $40,937.

Bucketed risk bumps one point of the curve at a time (2y, 5y, 10y, ...) instead of the whole curve. It shows where on the curve you are exposed. A 5y-vs-10y steepener can have zero total DV01 and large bucket risks.

7 · Probability and the normal table N(x)

Options pay only in some outcomes, so we need probability. The expected value is each outcome times its probability, added up. A ticket that pays £100 on heads is worth 0.5 × 100 = £50, or £48 if it pays in a year and D = 0.96.

For pricing we use risk-neutral probabilities. They are the probabilities that make today's market prices consistent, since anyone can hedge. In practice this means: assume the underlying grows on average at its forward, take the expected payoff, and discount it.

Many moves in markets look like a bell curve. N(x) is the probability that a standard bell-curve variable ends up below x. You can read it from a table, or from the chart below.

8 · Volatility: lognormal or normal

Volatility σ measures how much the underlying wiggles, quoted per year. Uncertainty grows with the square root of time. 20% annual vol means a one-standard-deviation move over 3 months is 20% × √0.25 = 10%.

  • Lognormal vol (Black) is in percent of the price. Use it for stocks, FX, commodities and bond futures, which cannot go below zero.
  • Normal vol (Bachelier) is in absolute units, such as "90bp a year". Rates desks quote swaption and cap vols this way because rates can be negative and move by similar amounts whatever their level.

9 · Black-76 and Bachelier: the two option formulas

A call pays max(ST − K, 0) at expiry. A put pays max(K − ST, 0). Both formulas below follow the same recipe: take the expected payoff under a bell-curve assumption around the forward F, then multiply by the discount factor. Almost every option in this course uses one of them.

Black-76 (lognormal), on a forward F Call = D × [ F N(d1) − K N(d2) ]    Put = D × [ K N(−d2) − F N(−d1) ]
d1 = ln(F/K) + ½σ2Tσ√T    d2 = d1 − σ√T
Bachelier (normal), on a forward F Call = D × [ (F − K) N(d) + σN√T n(d) ]    Put = D × [ (K − F) N(−d) + σN√T n(d) ]
d = F − KσN√T    n(d) = e−d²/2√(2π)
F
forward price of the underlying for the expiry date
K
strike
T
years to expiry
σ, σN
lognormal vol (e.g. 0.20) and normal vol (e.g. 0.0100 = 100bp)
D
discount factor to the payment date
N(d2)
for a Black call, the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money

Put-call parity checks any option price: Call − Put = D × (F − K). A call and a short put with the same strike are together a forward.

  1. Black call: F = 100, K = 105, T = 1, σ = 20%, D = 0.96.
    d1 = (ln(100/105) + 0.5×0.04) / 0.20 = −0.14395 d2 = −0.34395
    N(d1) = 0.44277 N(d2) = 0.36544 Call = 0.96 × (100×0.44277 − 105×0.36544) = 5.6694
  2. Parity check: Put = Call − D(F − K) = 5.6694 + 4.80 = 10.4694.
  3. Bachelier payer on a rate: F = 4.00%, K = 4.25%, T = 1, σN = 100bp, D = 0.96.
    d = (4.00 − 4.25) / 1.00 = −0.25 N(d) = 0.40129 n(d) = 0.38667
    Call = 0.96 × (−0.25×0.40129 + 1.00×0.38667) = 0.2749% of notional per year of accrual

Black call 5.6694, put 10.4694. Bachelier call 0.2749%. Switch the model in the calculator to check the second one.

Remember

  • PV = Σ cashflow × discount factor. Everything else is working out the cashflows.
  • Quote rates with their compounding and day count, or they mean nothing.
  • Forwards come from ratios of discount factors on the projection curve. Discounting uses OIS.
  • Linear products: replace unknown rates and prices by forwards. Options: use Black (lognormal) or Bachelier (normal) around the forward.
  • Greeks are bump-and-reprice "what ifs": delta, gamma, vega, theta, DV01, CS01.
