Part 1 · Cash products

Shares, preferreds & warrants

The most vanilla product of all. A share is priced by the market every second, so its PV is just quantity times price. The work is in currencies, dividends and the share-like products built on top of it.

Level 1 The idea

A company is split into millions of equal pieces called shares. Owning one share means owning a tiny slice of everything the company has and will earn. If the company pays out some profit, you get your slice as a dividend. If people think the company will earn more in future, they pay more for each slice and the share price rises.

Two cousins live on the same page because they are priced from the share:

  • A preferred share pays a fixed dividend, like a bond that never matures. It ranks ahead of ordinary shares if the company gets into trouble.
  • A warrant is a right, issued by the company itself, to buy a new share at a fixed price before a deadline. It is a call option with a twist.

A share is a slice of a pizza that keeps growing. A preferred share is a coupon promising you one fixed slice a year. A warrant is a voucher to buy a slice later at today's agreed price. That voucher is valuable only if the pizza gets more expensive.

Level 2 The cashflows

You pay the price on the settlement date. While you hold the share you receive dividends, whose size is not promised. When you sell, you receive whatever the market price is then.

A warrant has just two cashflows: the premium you pay today and, if you exercise, the share (worth ST) in exchange for the strike K. You only exercise when ST > K.

Level 3 The PV formula

Ordinary shares (type Equity)

In theory a share is worth all its future dividends discounted. If the next dividend is D1, dividends grow at g a year and investors want a return k, the sum of that endless list is the Gordon formula P = D1 ÷ (k − g). That explains why prices move. For pricing a position we never use it, because the exchange already tells us the price.

Step 1 · value in the share's own currency PVlocal = q × S
Step 2 · convert to the book's base currency PVUSD = q × SX    (where X = local units per 1 USD, e.g. USDJPY = 150)
q
number of shares (negative if short)
S
last price or closing price on the exchange
X
FX rate quoted as local currency per USD. Some pairs (EURUSD, GBPUSD) are quoted the other way, so multiply instead.

When you need the share's value at a future date (for a forward or a future), carry it forward at the funding rate r and take off the dividends you will miss, at a yield yd:

Equity forward price F(T) = S × e(r − yd)T    or, with known cash dividends,    F(T) = (S − PV(dividends)) ÷ D(T)

Preferred shares (type PreferredStock)

A fixed dividend d paid forever, discounted at the yield y investors demand, adds up to a strikingly simple number. Most preferreds can be called (bought back) by the issuer at par, usually $25, so the price struggles to go much above par.

Perpetual preferred, before call risk P = dy     with a call at par:   P ≈ min( dy , call price )

Warrants (type Warrant)

A warrant is a call option, so it is priced with Black-Scholes (see Options & Black-Scholes and the toolkit). The twist is dilution. When warrants are exercised the company prints M new shares on top of its N existing ones, which shrinks every slice. The standard fix scales the option value down.

Warrant value per warrant (one share per warrant) W = NN + M × [ Se−ydT N(d1) − Ke−rT N(d2) ]
d1 = ln(S/K) + (r − yd + ½σ2)Tσ√T   d2 = d1 − σ√T

Level 4 Desk reality

  • Settlement. US and Canadian shares settle T+1 (since May 2024). Europe and most of Asia settle T+2. China A-shares bought through Stock Connect deliver the shares on T and the cash on T+1. The position counts from trade date. Cash moves on settlement date.
  • Which price. End-of-day PV uses the official close. A position quoted in pence (GBp) or in lots needs scaling: LSE prices are in pence, so divide by 100.
  • Dividends. On the ex-date the price drops by roughly the dividend and a receivable appears instead. Total PV does not jump.
  • Preferreds in practice. FNMA V8.25 PERP S is a Fannie Mae preferred. Its dividends have been suspended since the 2008 conservatorship, so its price reflects legal and political outcomes, and the formula above does not apply. Live payers price off d/y and their call date.
  • PayToHold. Desks book these alongside some positions, for example PayToHold Open YUANJIE SEMICO-A CG. They carry the cost of holding a position, such as a financing or access fee. Price each fee like a single cashflow: amount × discount factor.

Worked example

A USD fund holds 10,000 shares of a Tokyo-listed stock. The close is JPY 3,250 and USDJPY is 150.00.

  1. Local value.
    PV_JPY = 10,000 × 3,250 = JPY 32,500,000
  2. Convert to USD (USDJPY is yen per dollar, so divide).
    PV_USD = 32,500,000 / 150 = USD 216,667
  3. Equity delta: the stock rises 1%.
    32,825,000 / 150 = 218,833 → +USD 2,167
  4. FX delta: the yen weakens 1% (USDJPY 151.50).
    32,500,000 / 151.5 = 214,521 → −USD 2,145
  5. Preferred: $25 par, 8.25% dividend ($2.0625 a year), required yield 9%.
    P = 2.0625 / 0.09 = $22.92 (below the $25 call price, so the call does not bind)
  6. Warrant: S = 10, K = 12, 3 years, σ = 40%, r = 3%, no dividends, 100m shares and 10m warrants.
    d1 = (ln(10/12) + (0.03 + 0.08)×3) / (0.40×√3) = 0.21316 d2 = −0.47966
    Call = 10×N(0.21316) − 12e^(−0.09)×N(−0.47966) = 2.3813 W = 2.3813 × 100/110 = 2.1648

The share position is worth USD 216,667. It moves about $2,167 per 1% in the stock and $2,145 per 1% in the yen. The preferred is worth $22.92 and the warrant 2.1648.

Remember

  • Share PV = quantity × price ÷ FX. There is no model, because the market prices it.
  • A foreign share carries two risks: the stock (delta) and the currency (FX delta).
  • The forward price of a share is spot grown at the funding rate minus the dividends.
  • Preferred ≈ dividend ÷ yield, capped near the call price.
  • Warrant = Black-Scholes call × N/(N + M) dilution factor.
