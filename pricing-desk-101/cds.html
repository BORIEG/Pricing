Part 4 · Credit

Credit default swaps

Insurance against a company going bust. One side pays a small premium every quarter. The other side pays a large amount only if the company defaults. Pricing it means estimating how likely that default is.

Level 1 The idea

You lend USD 10 million to Xerox by buying its bonds. If Xerox goes bankrupt, you may only get 40 cents back on each dollar. You want insurance against that.

A credit default swap (CDS) is that insurance. The protection buyer pays a fixed premium (the coupon, for example 1% a year) every quarter. The protection seller promises that if Xerox defaults, it pays the loss: the notional minus what the bonds are still worth after default.

A default here means a credit event: the company fails to pay, goes bankrupt, or (for some contracts) restructures its debt. After default the bonds still trade at some price, called the recovery rate R. A typical guess for senior debt is 40%.

It works like car insurance. You pay a small premium each month. If you crash, the insurer pays for the damage. If you never crash, the insurer keeps the premiums. The insurer prices the policy from how likely you are to crash and how much a crash costs.

Nobody has to own the bonds to trade a CDS. A seller of protection is simply taking credit risk on Xerox, much like buying its bond. The example trade Sell XRX SNRFOR USD 100 Dec 2027 is exactly that. We sell protection on Xerox senior unsecured debt (SNRFOR). The running coupon is 100bp (1%) and the contract ends on 20 Dec 2027.

Level 2 The cashflows

There are two legs.

  • The premium leg. The buyer pays coupon × notional × τ on each standard quarterly date (20 Mar, 20 Jun, 20 Sep, 20 Dec), where τ is the year fraction ACT/360. Payments stop at default. On default the buyer also pays the coupon accrued since the last payment date.
  • The protection leg. If default happens before maturity, the seller pays (1 − R) × notional once, and the contract ends. If no default happens, this leg pays nothing.

Because both legs depend on survival, we need one new ingredient beyond discount factors: the probability that the company is still alive at each date.

Level 3 The PV formula

Step 1 · Survival probability

Assume the company defaults at a steady rate λ (the hazard rate). λ = 7.6% means roughly a 7.6% chance of defaulting over the next year, given it is alive today. The probability of surviving to time t is then:

Survival probabilityQ(t) = e−λ t

Step 2 · The credit triangle (quick estimate)

The fair premium s (the par spread) must pay for the expected loss. Expected loss per year is the default rate times the loss per default:

Credit triangles ≈ λ × (1 − R) ⇔ λ ≈ s1 − R

For Xerox at 450bp with 40% recovery: λ ≈ 0.045 ÷ 0.6 = 7.5%. The exact value solved below is 7.60%.

Step 3 · Value each leg

Each premium is weighted by the chance of receiving it and discounted with the OIS curve D(t). On a default inside a period, the buyer still pays accrued coupon. That is worth about half a period on average, so we weight period i by the average survival of its two ends. The resulting sum is the risky annuity, or RPV01: the PV of receiving 1 per year while the name survives.

Risky annuity (premium leg per unit of coupon) RPV01 = Σi τi D(ti) Q(ti−1) + Q(ti)2
Protection leg (per unit notional) Prot = (1 − R) Σi D(ti) [ Q(ti−1) − Q(ti) ]

Step 4 · Par spread, upfront and PV

Putting it together par spread s = Prot ÷ RPV01
upfront paid by the buyer (% of notional) = Prot − c × RPV01
PVseller = N × ( c × RPV01 − Prot ) ≈ N × (c − s) × RPV01
c
the fixed running coupon of the contract (100bp or 500bp)
s
the market par spread, the coupon that would make the contract worth zero
ti, τi
payment dates (years ACT/365 from today) and accrual fractions (ACT/360)
N
notional

The market quotes a spread, but the contract pays a fixed coupon. The difference is settled as an upfront cash payment, roughly (s − c) × RPV01. To price a CDS: find the hazard rate λ that reproduces the quoted spread, then value both legs with that λ.

Recovery swaps (type RecoverySwap)

A recovery swap (or recovery lock) bets only on the recovery rate. There are no premiums. If default happens, the recovery buyer receives (Rrealised − Rfixed) × N, which can be negative. If no default happens, nothing is paid.

Recovery swap, recovery buyer PV = N × (Rexpected − Rfixed) × Σi D(ti) [ Q(ti−1) − Q(ti) ]

The sum is the protection leg divided by (1 − R). With the Xerox numbers below (0.045852 ÷ 0.6 = 0.076420), a USD 10m lock struck at 35% when the market expects 40% is worth 10m × 5% × 0.076420 = USD 38,210 to the recovery buyer.

Level 4 Desk reality

  • Standard contracts. Since 2009 CDS trade with a fixed coupon of 100bp (investment grade) or 500bp (high yield). Maturities fall on the IMM dates 20 Mar, 20 Jun, 20 Sep or 20 Dec, so all trades in one name are fungible. That is why the trade description ends with Dec 2027, not an exact date.
  • ISDA standard model. Dealers convert spread to upfront with the same simple model everyone uses: a flat hazard rate, the ISDA OIS-based discount curve, recovery 40% for senior (25% for subordinated), and protection starting today. Our Step 3 is a close cousin. ISDA integrates the default timing exactly, so its numbers differ by a few cents per 100.
  • Accrued at trade. The buyer pays a full coupon on the next IMM date. At trade the seller pays back the accrued coupon since the last IMM date. The net effect is premium accruing from today, which is what our first short period (27 days) models.
  • Upfront settles T+3 business days. Premiums are ACT/360 and the last period includes the maturity day.
  • Reference obligation & seniority. SNRFOR means senior unsecured foreign debt. SUBLT2 would be subordinated, with lower recovery and a wider spread. Restructuring clauses (CR, MR, MM14, XR14) change which events trigger, so the same name can have several curves.
  • After default an ISDA auction fixes the final recovery price. Every CDS settles at (1 − auction price) × notional.
  • Real curves. The hazard rate is bootstrapped from quotes at 6M, 1Y, 2Y, 3Y, 5Y, 7Y and 10Y, so it varies by period. Our flat λ is the one-quote version.

Worked example

Today is 23 Nov 2026. You have sold USD 10m of protection on Xerox at a 100bp coupon, maturing 20 Dec 2027. The market quotes Xerox at a 450bp par spread. We assume R = 40% and a flat USD OIS rate of 4.00% (continuous, ACT/365).

  1. Payment dates and fractions. Accrual starts today.
    20 Dec 26: 27d τ=0.07500 t=0.07397 20 Mar 27: 90d τ=0.25000 t=0.32055 20 Jun 27: 92d τ=0.25556 t=0.57260 20 Sep 27: 92d τ=0.25556 t=0.82466 20 Dec 27: 91d τ=0.25278 t=1.07397
  2. Guess λ with the credit triangle (0.045 ÷ 0.6 = 7.50%). Then adjust until Prot ÷ RPV01 equals exactly 450bp. The solver finds:
    λ = 7.6044% Q(1.07397) = e^(−0.076044×1.07397) = 0.921577
  3. Discount factors D = e−0.04t and survival per date:
    D: 0.997045 0.987260 0.977356 0.967552 0.957951 Q: 0.994391 0.975919 0.957391 0.939216 0.921577
  4. Risky annuity. For example, the first term is 0.075 × 0.997045 × (1 + 0.994391)/2 = 0.074569.
    RPV01 = 0.074569 + 0.243151 + 0.241440 + 0.234481 + 0.225294 = 1.018935
  5. Protection leg. For example, the first term is 0.6 × 0.997045 × (1 − 0.994391) = 0.003356.
    Prot = 0.003356 + 0.010942 + 0.010865 + 0.010552 + 0.010138 = 0.045852 check: 0.045852 / 1.018935 = 4.500%
  6. Upfront and PV for the seller.
    upfront = 0.045852 − 0.01 × 1.018935 = 0.035663 → 3.566% of notional PV seller = 10,000,000 × (0.01 × 1.018935 − 0.045852) = −USD 356,627
  7. Risk, by bumping and repricing:
    CS01 = −USD 987 per bp Rec01 = +USD 241 JTD = −6,000,000 + 356,627 = −USD 5,643,373

The short-protection position is worth −USD 356,627. Equivalently, a new seller would be paid 3.57 points upfront to accept a 100bp coupon on a name the market prices at 450bp.

Remember

  • A CDS swaps a running premium for a one-off payment of (1 − R) × notional on default.
  • Survival Q(t) = e−λt, and λ ≈ spread ÷ (1 − R).
  • PVseller = N(c × RPV01 − Prot) ≈ N(c − s) × RPV01. The upfront settles the gap between coupon and spread.
